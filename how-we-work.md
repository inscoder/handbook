# How We Work

## Remote is the Default

We have been remote since Day 1\. We don’t do this because it’s trendy. We do it because it’s a better way to work.

It allows us to hire great people anywhere in the world, not just those within a 30-minute commute of an office.

### Your Setup Matters

Work from anywhere, as long as you can actually work.

High-speed internet is essential. If your connection stutters during meetings or cuts you off from the team-even at home-that location doesn't cut it. Be professional about your tools.

### Moving Around

You are free to move between cities or countries, but talk to us before you pack.

Time zones are real physics. We need enough overlap to collaborate without anyone having to wake up at 3:00 AM. Just keep us in the loop so we know when-and where-to find you.

## Physical Workspaces

We rent a private office at Regus in Hong Kong. It holds 2 to 4 people, and there is a large public area you can use.

This is our mailing address and a spot for face-to-face work. You can also book a "community room" for free (two hours a day) via the Regus app. You are welcome to use it whenever you like.

We also have Regus Global memberships. This gives you access to over 3,000 locations worldwide. If you need a desk somewhere else, search for it here: \[[Link](https://regus.com/)\].

## Work Cycles

We organize internal work into 8-week cycles. This gives us six clean starts every year.

This structure creates a predictable rhythm. All the planning happens before the cycle starts, so when Day 1 arrives, there is no ambiguity. We know exactly what we are building.

Crucially, this protects our focus. We don't shove new ideas into the middle of a cycle. We commit to a scope and we finish it.

This constraint forces us to be picky. We have limited capacity, so we debate hard about what makes the cut. If a project isn't worth the time, we say no.

This method is inspired by **Shape Up**. You can read the full guide here: \[[Link](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/0.3-chapter-01#six-week-cycles)\].

## Communication

### Write and Wait

We write more than we talk. We do this to protect your focus.

If we need something, we write it down. We don't expect an instant reply. We expect you to get to it when you are ready. This is "Write and Wait." It allows us to work for long stretches without being interrupted by pings and taps on the shoulder.

We still talk-especially for design feedback, complex troubleshooting, or 1-on-1s-but real-time is the exception, not the default.

Read our full philosophy here: \[[Link](https://3.basecamp.com/3926929/buckets/6210089/messages/8281051554)\]

### Radiate Information

In a remote team, silence creates anxiety. We can't see you at your desk, so if we don't hear from you, we wonder if you're stuck.

Don't wait to be asked. "Radiate" information. Document your progress publicly so the team knows where you stand without having to interrupt you to ask.

We have a field guide on how to use tools like Hill Charts and To-Do comments to keep everyone in the loop without saying a word. Read it here: \[[Link](https://3.basecamp.com/3926929/buckets/6210089/messages/8307612227)\].

In addition to your daily work, we rely on four automated prompts to keep the rhythm:

* **Weekly Plan:** Monday morning. Share what you intend to get done.

* **Daily Progress:** Twice a week. Don't just list tasks; explain *why* the work matters.

* **Cycle Kickoff:** The roadmap for the next 8 weeks.

* **Cycle Heartbeat:** A celebration of what we finished in the last cycle.

### 1-on-1 Meetings

The 1-on-1 is the most important hour on your calendar.

This is not a status meeting. Do not use it to read down a checklist of tasks. This time is for high-leverage work: unblocking challenges, career growth, and mutual teaching.

**You own the agenda.** Send topics a day in advance so we can prepare. We treat this meeting as a flexible tool; we adjust the frequency based on what you need right now, rather than sticking to a rigid calendar.

To make the best use of this time, we recommend these two resources:

* Chapter 4 of Andrew Grove's *High Output Management*  
* Chapter 8 of Kim Scott’s *Radical Candor* 

## Managers of One

We don't have full-time managers. We probably never will.

Everyone here does real work. No one's job is just to watch you do yours. That means we don't micromanage. We trust you to manage yourself.

We expect you to set your own direction. You identify what needs to be done, and then you do it-without waiting to be told.

People who do this well are called [Managers of One](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/1430-hire-managers-of-one).

To be blunt: If you need someone to hand you a task list every morning, you might succeed somewhere else, but you won't last long here.

## Work Intensity

A typical week is 40 hours.

Sometimes a deadline requires 50\.  Over Time (OT) happens. When it does, balance it with a lighter week later. If life forces you to work less, simply make it up. We **trust** you to manage the flow yourself.

When you need to officially book that downtime (provided you’ve first completed your standard 40 hours), follow the \[[Compensation Leave Policy](https://3.basecamp.com/3926929/buckets/40447328/documents/8169457054)\].

Standard hours in HK/China are 9:00 AM to 6:00 PM.

If you are elsewhere, we will set a schedule that creates enough overlap to collaborate.

We aren't clock-watchers. Running 30 minutes late isn't a crime. Occasional variances are fine, but habitual changes-like deciding to always start at 2:00 PM-need to be cleared with us first.

If we sense friction, we won't wait for a performance review. We will have a direct, Radical Candor conversation to fix it immediately. Ultimately, you are evaluated on the speed and quality of your work, not the hours you sit in a chair.

## Stepping Away

Life happens. Sometimes the plumber arrives early, the school calls, or you just need to run an errand in the middle of the day. That is fine. We trust you to manage your time.

However, disappearing without a word creates confusion. If you need to step away, don’t keep it a mystery. Drop a quick note in the [team chat](https://3.basecamp.com/3926929/buckets/6210089/chats/8252298213) to announce you’re leaving, and crucially, let us know when you expect to be back. It’s not about asking for permission; it’s about basic etiquette. It saves your teammates from wondering why you aren't replying and keeps the work moving while you’re gone.

## Deadlines

By default, we don't miss deadlines. Whether it’s internal work or a client deliverable, missing a date breaks trust and puts the business at stake. We take our commitments seriously.

But meeting a deadline isn't just about the date on the calendar; it's about the time of day. If a teammate is waiting on your output to do their job, finishing at 9:00 PM creates a bottleneck. You might have technically hit the deadline, but you’ve forced them to wait all day-or worse, work late-to pick up where you left off.

Be considerate of the handoff. Aim to finish your part by noon so others have time to act on it. If you suspect you might miss a deadline, communicate it immediately. Bad news must travel fast.

## We Don’t Accept Gifts

Please don’t accept gifts. They usually end up unused, donated, or thrown away anyway.

If someone hints at sending one, politely tell them it’s unnecessary.

Low-value courtesy items sometimes show up anyway: mooncakes, company souvenirs, notebooks, and similar things. If refusing would be awkward, receive them on behalf of the company, tell your manager, and share or use them for the team where practical. Anything valuable, repeated, cash-like, or tied to a business decision must be declined or escalated first.

Product samples sent for work, testing, or helping us better understand a client’s business are fine.
